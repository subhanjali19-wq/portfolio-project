# EP 251 — Google Lied About Click Data: Here's What Actually Drives Rankings in 2026 (with Cyrus Shepard)
**Speaker:** Cyrus Shepard (interviewed by David Mihm and Mike Blumenthal, Near Memo / Near Media)
**Publication Date:** April 9, 2026
**YouTube URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rnLbyvn3L2s

---
## Transcript Summary
- Cyrus explains that Google's DOJ antitrust trial documents, an API data-warehouse leak, and long-standing patents together confirm Google uses click data — including "long clicks" and "last longest click" — as a major ranking signal, despite years of public denial.
- He describes "last longest click" as Google's proxy for task completion: a user clicks a result, spends meaningful time engaging, and does not return to the search results to try another option.
- Cyrus ties click-signal theory to local SEO, arguing that actions on Google Business Profiles (calls, direction requests, bookings) function as an equivalent conversion signal that businesses should consolidate rather than let third-party sites (e.g., Yelp) capture.
- He explains that brand search volume is a strong, consistently observed ranking correlate, and that Google's newly added branded-query filter in Search Console makes this easier to track.
- On AI Overviews, Cyrus notes Google reportedly built new lower-cost algorithms (referenced as "FastSearch," "FastRank," and "BedBERT" in the conversation) to generate AI answers cheaply for long-tail queries, relying more on machine-learning estimates of click behavior than fresh real-time click data.
- He argues AI Overviews primarily benefit Google itself, since many citation links route users back into further Google searches rather than out to publisher sites, and that Google is likely using AI/ML to replace much of the manual Quality Rater evaluation work he used to perform.
- Cyrus recommends brands actively "own" the content that appears in AI Overviews about themselves, since AI systems are "starving for content" on branded queries and often surface whatever a business publishes about itself with little competitive friction.
- He advises against manipulative title-tag optimization that earns clicks without matching user intent, since resulting "bad clicks" (users clicking back) send a negative signal; instead, he recommends matching title promises to page content, answering queries quickly near the top of the page, and using engagement-oriented page elements (navigation, related content) to increase dwell time.
- He closes by recommending marketers track engagement metrics in GA4 (engaged sessions, time on site) as proxies for the click-satisfaction signals Google is believed to use, since direct measurement of "task completion" is not otherwise available to site owners.
